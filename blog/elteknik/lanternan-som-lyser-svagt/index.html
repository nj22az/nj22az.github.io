The navigation light that glows dimly

Ohm’s law and voltage drop in practice

Nils Johansson · 23 September 2026 · 3 min read

It is evening, and the starboard navigation light is noticeably dimmer than the port light. The batteries are charged and the multimeter reads 24 V at the distribution board. You disconnect the lamp and measure at the light's terminals: 24 V there too. Yet it glows dimly. Where is the voltage going?

The cable has resistance too

The answer lies in Ohm's law, U = R · I. Every conductor has resistance, and current flowing through it produces a voltage drop. For a copper conductor:

R = ρ · L / A

For copper, ρ is approximately 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m, L is the conductor length and A its cross-sectional area. Remember that the current travels out and back: a 40-metre cable run to the light means 80 metres of conductor. With 1.5 mm² conductors:

Rcable = 0.0175 · 80 / 1.5 ≈ 0.93 Ω

Calculate the whole circuit

Assume an incandescent lamp rated at 24 V / 48 W. At its rated voltage it draws 2 A, corresponding to 24 / 2 = 12 Ω. A filament's resistance changes with temperature, but this is sufficient as an estimate. The lamp and cable are in series:

Total resistance12 + 0.93 = 12.93 Ω
Current24 / 12.93 ≈ 1.86 A
Voltage across the lamp1.86 · 12 ≈ 22.3 V
Lamp power1.86² · 12 ≈ 41 W, about 86% of rated power

Even a reasonably sized cable therefore costs almost two volts. But the port light has the same cable run and shines more brightly. There is something else in the starboard circuit.

The corroded terminal

A terminal starting to oxidise in a damp junction box can develop a contact resistance of a few tenths of an ohm. Add 0.5 Ω in series:

Total resistance12 + 0.93 + 0.5 = 13.43 Ω
Current24 / 13.43 ≈ 1.79 A
Voltage across the terminal1.79 · 0.5 ≈ 0.89 V
Voltage across the lamp≈ 21.4 V
Lamp power≈ 38 W, about 80% of rated power

A fifth of the light has been lost, and almost one volt is dropped across a single screwed connection. The power dissipated there, just under 1.6 W, becomes heat in the terminal, and that heat accelerates corrosion.

Why the measurement misled you

With the lamp disconnected, no current flows. Then I = 0, and the voltage drop R · I across every resistance in the circuit is zero, however poor the terminal is. The voltmeter shows the full battery voltage: a correct measurement of the wrong thing.

Measure under load instead, with the lamp lit:

  1. Measure the voltage directly across the lamp connections.
  2. Measure across each connection along the route. A sound connection should read close to 0 V.
  3. Compare these readings with the voltage at the fuse in the distribution board.

The voltage drops around the loop add up to the supply voltage. This is Kirchhoff's voltage law, and it identifies exactly where the volts are being lost.

Key points

  • Calculate cable resistance for the entire loop, out and back.
  • Voltage drop appears only when current flows. Measure under load.
  • A poor connection reveals itself as a voltage drop across something that should be 0 Ω.
