The insulation alarm that will not clear

How a shipboard IT system works

Nils Johansson · 23 September 2026 · 3 min read

The low-insulation-resistance alarm for the 440 V system sounds in the control room. You acknowledge it, and a little later it returns. Nothing has tripped and all the equipment is running. How serious is it?

A system without an earthed neutral point

On shore, most low-voltage supplies are earthed: the neutral point is connected to earth, and an earth fault produces a short circuit that trips the protective device. On board, the main supply is often an IT system, with the neutral point insulated from the hull.

At the first earth fault, when one phase contacts the hull, there is no closed return path to the source apart from small capacitive leakage currents through cables and equipment. No fuse operates. The steering gear, pumps and lighting continue to run.

Why it is used

That is the point: a single fault should not shut down systems the vessel needs. The trade-off is that the fault must be detected another way. An insulation monitoring device therefore continuously monitors the resistance between the system and the hull, and raises an alarm when it falls below a set value.

Why acknowledging the alarm does not clear it

The alarm describes a condition, not an event. Acknowledging it confirms that someone has seen it, but while the insulation resistance remains low, the cause remains. The alarm clears only when the fault has gone.

The second fault

An earth fault on one phase causes the two healthy phases to rise to full line voltage relative to the hull: 440 V rather than approximately 254 V. This places greater stress on the insulation in the rest of the system.

If a second earth fault then occurs on another phase, it becomes a phase-to-phase short circuit through the hull. Protective devices will operate, possibly in two circuits at once, and perhaps in a system that must keep running. An earth fault in an IT system must therefore be found and corrected promptly, rather than merely recorded in the log.

Finding the fault

  1. Follow the reading. Did the insulation resistance fall suddenly or gradually? Does it follow equipment starting or stopping, such as a heater or a deck motor?
  2. Section the system. Disconnect one circuit at a time, in a planned and coordinated way, and watch for the reading to recover. Some installations have earth-fault indication for each circuit, and a leakage-current clamp can help narrow down the fault.
  3. Measure with the circuit dead. Once the circuit is identified, isolate it using the established procedure and measure its insulation resistance with an insulation tester. First disconnect electronics that could be damaged by the test voltage.
  4. Look for the usual causes. Moisture in junction boxes and deck motors, damaged cable entries and heating elements.

Key points

  • In an IT system, the first earth fault raises an alarm rather than tripping the supply.
  • An alarm that returns means the fault is still present.
  • Find the fault before a second one occurs.
