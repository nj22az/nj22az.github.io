The pump that will not start

Systematic fault-finding, step by step

Nils Johansson · 23 September 2026 · 3 min read

The pump is to be started at its starter. You press Start, and nothing happens: no click, no hum. It is easy to replace the component you suspect, but a systematic approach beats guesswork, especially when time is short.

1. Describe the symptom precisely

What happens, and what does not? Does the contactor click? Does an indicator light come on? Has the pump worked before, and what has happened since: maintenance, alarms or a power failure? A precise description often rules out half the installation before you even open a cover.

2. Read the diagram and split the system

A motor starter has two circuits that can be investigated separately:

  • The power circuit: supply, fuses or circuit-breaker, the contactor's main contacts, overload protection and motor.
  • The control circuit: control supply, the stop chain with emergency stops, the overload relay's auxiliary contact and any level or pressure switches, the start control and the contactor coil.

3. Narrow it down

If the contactor does not pull in, the fault lies in the control circuit; the motor is not yet involved. Then divide the circuit in half: measure the control voltage halfway along the stop chain. If it is present, the fault lies after the test point; if not, it lies before it. A few such divisions can locate an open contact even in a long chain.

If the contactor pulls in but the motor does not run, the fault lies in the power circuit. Check the voltage on all three phases after the contactor. A missing phase often makes the motor hum without starting.

4. Measure safely

Control circuits are often investigated while energised. That requires an instrument suitable for the measurement category, appropriate test leads and a plan for placing the probe tips. Check the instrument against a known voltage before and after testing. All work that can be carried out dead should be carried out dead.

Suppose the readings show that the voltage is lost across the overload relay's auxiliary contact: the protection has tripped. This is where many investigations end too early.

5. Find the cause, not just the fault

The overload protection tripped for a reason. A binding pump, a lost phase or an incorrect protection-current setting can all produce the same symptom. Reset the protection, start under supervision and measure the current in all three phases. Compare the readings with the nameplate and with each other. An imbalance between phases, or current above the rated value, tells you more than the trip alone.

Finish by verifying that the pump runs correctly, and document what you found. The next person standing in front of that starter will benefit from your notes.

Key points

  • Describe the symptom before measuring.
  • Separate the power and control circuits, then divide the search.
  • A tripped protective device is a symptom. Find the cause before resetting it and leaving.
