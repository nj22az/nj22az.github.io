# Classic car and passenger ferries, 1958 to 1978: real ships in three sizes

This reference was gathered on 11 November 2026 and written up on 12 November 2026 by a research agent from public
sources. Every figure names its source through the bracketed keys listed below. "n/v" means not verified: either no
source reached gave the figure, or the only basis is an inference that is stated as such. Nothing here is invented to
fill a gap.

## How to read this

Source keys used in tables and text. Full URLs are in section 7.

- [F] faktaomfartyg.se ship page (Swedish ship database). Its dimension line is printed as three unlabelled figures,
  normally length x breadth x draught. Tonnage is printed as Brt/Nrt/Dwt.
- [P] my own reading of a photograph on a faktaomfartyg photo page. Lifeboat counts from photos are plus or minus one.
- [GA] my own reading of the builder's general arrangement drawing of Stena Danica (1965), four sheets on
  faktaomfartyg. The scan is low resolution; room labels and dimensions were read from enlargements.
- [B] Sessanlinjen brochure page with deck plans of Prinsessan Christina and Prinsessan Désirée, on faktaomfartyg.
- [D] doverferryphotosforums.co.uk "past and present" ship page.
- [L] Länspumpen (Swedish maritime history journal), monthly ship article by Anders Bergenek.
- [S] shipstamps.co.uk forum post. [FS] ferry-site.dk vessel page.
- [TA] Transportation Safety Board of Canada report M19C0054 (Apollo, 2019).
- [TQ] Transportation Safety Board of Canada report M06W0052 (Queen of the North, ex Stena Danica of 1969).
- [E] Final report on the loss of Estonia (1997), chapter 3 "The vessel" (ship built 1980 by Meyer as Viking Sally).
- [Wsv] [Wfi] [Wen] [Wde] Wikipedia in Swedish, Finnish, English, German.
- [LX] lex.dk (Danish encyclopedia), article "bilfærge".
- [I] IMO background paper "Surviving disaster: life-saving at sea" (January 2000).
- [A] acts.co.za reproduction of SOLAS Chapter III in its pre-1983 form (schedule to the 1974 convention).
- [V] viking-life.com article on the Skagerak rescue. [K] klikk.no article on Skagerak.
- [M] mmncny.org museum page on ship electrical supplies. [ML] MarineLink news item, seen as a search summary only.

Where a table row gives several source keys in its last column, a figure without its own key comes from the first key.

Notes on the small class:

- Stena Danica (1965): [F] prints 86.04 x 16.65 x 3.90 m. The builder's drawing [GA] gives length overall 79.60 m,
  length between perpendiculars 72.00 m and breadth at car deck 16.20 m, which agrees with [D] and [L]. I take [F]
  to be wrong on length.
- Stena Danica (1965): [D] prints "6,000 kW". 6,000 metric horsepower is 4,413 kW, so [D] most likely means hp.
- Her car deck is 5.40 m above base and her promenade deck 10.10 m [GA], which gives 4.70 m moulded from deck to
  deck. Clear headroom under the beams is n/v. Her car deck was built 35 cm higher than the Sessan ferries' [L].
- Stena had no experience of car ferries in 1963 and gave these two ships a similar design and the same dimensions
  as Scania, then building at Uddevallavarvet for the Svea group [L].
- Regula's auxiliaries: five Volvo Penta TMD 100A of 140 kW each [F]. The KaMeWa designations are as printed in [F];
  reading "2200" as a 2,200 mm controllable-pitch propeller is my interpretation.
- Betula, Regula and Ursula worked Helsingborg to Helsingør and had no cabins at all [F]. They were rebuilt at
  Helsingør in 1984 to 1985 with a new car deck, which raised the car figure from 75 to 105 [F, Wsv].

Notes on the middle class:

- Apollo (1970) was the first of nine near-identical "Papenburg sisters" built by Meyer between 1969 and 1974
  (meyerwerft.de, seen as a search summary). She cost SEK 25 million and had ice class 1A [Wen].
- Apollo's power: [F] prints 6,968 kW for Apollo and Viking 1 but 5,968 kW for the sister Diana. 8,000 bhp is 5,968
  kW, and [D] gives exactly that pair for Viking 1. I take 6,968 to be a misprint for 5,968.
- Apollo per [TA]: nine decks in total including one vehicle deck; a bow visor that is raised and lowered; a vehicle
  deck running fore to aft with no transverse or longitudinal watertight bulkheads; two semi-balanced rudders;
  designed for 1,200 persons. Her 2019 thruster, generators (three sets of 500 kW) and engines are later equipment
  or later ratings, so they are evidence of the arrangement but not of the 1970 fit.
- "Depth 6.0 m" for Apollo and Viking 1 [D, S, Wen] is most likely the moulded depth to the vehicle deck. No source
  says so in words, so that reading is n/v.
- Gotland's third figure of 5.36 m [F] is large for a 93 m ferry and may be depth, not draught. n/v.
- Skagerak sailed on 7 September 1966 with 47 crew and 97 passengers; a breaking sea destroyed her stern door and
  she sank about nine hours later [F]. [V] says 147 people. This is the only period crew figure found for a ship
  under 110 m, and it is a number on board on one day, not a stated complement.
- Skandia: a search summary of English Wikipedia describes her as the first ship in Finland with a roll-through car
  deck with aft and bow gates. Her photo shows no conventional funnel [P], see section 5.

Notes on the large class:

- Fennia had eight decks and room for 120 crew [Wfi]. She was re-engined in 1975 with four MaK 9M453AK of 8,825 kW
  [F]. [Wfi] prints the original Ruston output as 8,760 kW where [F] says 8,760 hp.
- Prinsessan Christina's eight engines were chosen "to give operational versatility" [D].
- Stena Danica (1969) per [TQ]: eight decks including the double bottom; main car deck with three lanes to port and
  two to starboard of an engine casing set slightly off the centreline; eleven transverse watertight bulkheads;

  conversion, she carried two 57-person motor lifeboats under gravity davits, thirty 25-person davit-launched
  rafts, a crew of 42 on board and a maximum of 50.
- Aallotar and Svea Regina were the first purpose-built cruise ferries on Stockholm to Helsinki; Aallotar cost SEK
  65 million [L, F].

## 2. Typical general arrangement of each class

### Small class, from the Stena Danica (1965) drawing [GA]

Keel upward:

- Double bottom: stores, refrigerated stores, trim tanks.
- Lower deck, aft to forward: steering gear; dry tanks and trim tanks; two-berth crew cabins; crew's mess and
  officers' mess with pantry; engineers' cabins; main engine room; auxiliary engine room with an "inclination tank"
  at each side; empty-package and tax-free shop stores; 18 berths for female staff and 24 passenger berths; stores;
  bow propeller machine room; chain locker; fore peak.
- Car deck: one clear drive-through space. Stairs, casings, a sprinkler control room and stores sit along both
  sides, with a few pillars on the centreline.
- Promenade deck: dining saloon aft (136 to 142 seats); hall with luggage shelves; hot and cold galley amidships;
  toilets; cafeteria saloon forward (224 to 253 seats) with a dance floor; open promenade round the bow.
- Boat deck: sun deck aft; "Bel-Air" saloon with dance floor; entrance hall and a chocolate and cigarette shop; bar
  saloon forward (137 seats); two lifeboats each side.
- Top: wheelhouse, funnel casing, masts.

Officers and crew lived below the car deck aft. The owner wanted the largest possible number of seats in revenue
spaces: restaurant, cafeteria, two cocktail bars and large tax-free sales areas. The interiors were by Robert
Tillberg, with fine woods on the bulkheads and strong textile colours [L]. The Helsingborg to Helsingør ferries of
the same size had no cabins [F].

### Middle class

- Apollo (1970): nine decks. Vehicle deck is deck 3; decks 1 and 2 lie below it; deck 5 is the first deck above
  the vehicle space and has companionways forward and hatches aft; decks 6 and 7 carry passenger spaces and the
  emergency generator room is on deck 7; the wheelhouse is enclosed with open bridge wings that carry their own
  control consoles; there are separate main and auxiliary engine rooms and an engine control room [TA]. The report
  mentions these decks in passing, so the stack between them is my inference and n/v.
- Skandia (1961): cinema, restaurants, a hairdresser, 136 cabin berths and 114 reclining seats [F, Wfi].
- Kapella (1967): car deck, hanging deck and boat deck are named; 18 extra berths for lorry drivers were added on
  the boat deck in 1967 [F].
- Viking 1 (1970): decks lettered, with D deck and E deck in way of the engine room [F].

### Large class

- Stena Danica (1969) [TQ]: deck 1 double bottom; deck 2 tween deck with crew cabins (passengers were also berthed
  there originally); deck 3 main car deck; deck 4 platform deck; deck 5 saloon deck with cafeteria, bar, galley and
  purser's office; deck 6 promenade deck with lounges and seating; deck 7 boat deck with officers amidships and the
  wheelhouse at the forward end; deck 8 ventilation deck. Engine rooms amidships, with main engine room, auxiliary
  engine room and workshop as separate compartments.
- Prinsessan Christina, restaurant deck [B], forward to aft: captain's saloon, bridge room, two conference rooms,
  Bar Princess; cafeteria; a reading room one stair up; Jutland dining room and manor saloon; drivers' saloon;
  large dining saloon; large bar. Her sister's boat deck has Bar Najad, cabins, a sauna and a reading room.
- Fennia [Wfi], top to bottom: sun deck, command deck, boat deck, restaurant deck, A deck, car deck, C deck, D
  deck. Rooms: 60-seat cinema, barber, hairdresser, playroom, smoking saloon, dining room, café, self-service shop,
  a lounge with about 100 armchairs, two pools and four saunas below the car deck.
- Aallotar and Svea Regina [L]: pool, sauna and nightclub below the car deck; simple passenger cabins and crew
  cabins on two decks along the car deck; C deck with cabins, entrance hall and a self-service shop; B deck with
  bar saloon forward, grill and pub amidships, galley, and restaurant aft; A deck with luxury cabins, a television
  or cinema saloon and a rest saloon.

### Casings, funnels and vehicle decks

- lex.dk says Romsø (1973) was built without a centre casing "like many privately owned car ferries from the
  1960s", so that all stairways, exhausts and the like are placed along the ship's sides [LX]. This fits the twin
  side funnels or exhaust posts seen on Fennia, Svea Regina, Tor Anglia and Prinsessan Christina [P], and the side
  casings on the Stena Danica (1965) drawing [GA].
- A casing slightly off the centreline also existed: Stena Danica (1969) [TQ] and Viking Sally (1980) [E].
- Every period ship for which a vehicle-deck description was found has one main drive-through deck. Extra car
  capacity came from side galleries (Stena Danica 1965 [D]), a hanging deck (Kapella [F], Aallotar [L]) or
  hoistable platforms (Stena Danica 1969 [TQ]). Many ferries had such hanging decks that are raised and lowered as
  needed [LX].
- No lower-hold garage was found on any period ship. A fixed second full-height vehicle deck was found only on the
  state-owned Arveprins Knud of 1963 [LX] and then on the Stena Danica of 1983 [L, article of February 2023].

## 3. Engine room facts of the era

- Main engines: two or four medium-speed diesels on twin shafts in every ship found. Makers recorded: KHD (Deutz),
  MAN, Atlas-MaK, B&W, Werkspoor, MWM, Wärtsilä-Sulzer, Jugo-Sulzer, Ruston, Ottensener Eisenwerk, SEMT-Pielstick
  PC2 and Nohab-Polar [F]. Prinsessan Christina and Prinsessan Désirée had eight engines each [F].
- Controllable-pitch propellers: KaMeWa units on Regula (1971) [F]; controllable-pitch propellers on Apollo (1970)
  [S, Wen], Stena Danica (1969) [TQ] and Prinsessan Désirée (1971) [Wen].
- Bow thrusters: a "bow propeller machine room" is drawn on Stena Danica (1965) [GA]; KaMeWa thruster on Regula
  [F]; Fennia [Wfi]; Prinsessan Désirée [Wen]; Apollo's tunnel unit of 478 kW with a fixed-blade propeller and
  three speed settings, controlled from the wheelhouse and both wing consoles, is her 2019 state [TA].
- Auxiliary engines: five Volvo Penta sets of 140 kW each on Regula [F]. Apollo in 2019 had three 500 kW
  three-phase sets feeding a main switchboard, of which two had to be on line for the thruster, plus an emergency
  set arranged to start automatically [TA].
- Heeling tanks: "inclination tanks" each side of the auxiliary engine room on Stena Danica (1965) [GA].
- Stabilisers: fins on Tor Anglia [D]; "automatic stabilisation" advertised for Prinsessan Christina and Désirée
  [B]; fins on Aallotar and Svea Regina, called a novelty in the Finland trade in 1972 [L]. The small 1965 Stena
  ships "moved in a lively way in a seaway", which passengers disliked [L].
- Rudders: two semi-balanced rudders on Apollo [TA].
- Automation: Kinkasan Maru (1961) is credited as the first ship with bridge control of the main engine and
  centralised monitoring [ML]. No source reached says whether any 1960s ferry in this list had an engine control
  room. n/v.
- Voltage: no source reached gives the voltage of a 1960s ferry. A museum page says European-built ships tended to
  generate 380 V, 50 Hz, three-phase, and that older ships used 220 V or 110 V direct current [M].

The nearest fully described plant is Viking Sally of 1980, two years outside the period [E]:

- Four medium-speed engines geared in pairs to two shafts, each with a 4.0 m controllable-pitch propeller. A
  combinator set engine speed and pitch together from levers on the bridge, on both bridge wings and in the engine
  control room.
- Classed for unmanned machinery space, but in practice the control room was always manned by one engineer and one
  motorman.
- Three-phase 380 V, 50 Hz from four diesel generator sets of 1,104 kW; 220 V single-phase through transformers for
  lighting; main switchboard in the engine control room; emergency generator in its own room high in the ship near
  the engine casing, starting automatically in about 15 seconds.
- Deck 0: bow thruster room forward, generator room, engine room and fuel purifier room aft. Deck 1: engine control
  room, workshop and main engine room in the aft half.
- Two ballast pumps of 300 m3/h; a pair of side heeling tanks with their own pump, worked from the deck office at
  the aft ramp and from the control room.
- Bow visor, ramps and car platforms supplied by von Tell: a hydraulic system with three pumps, originally set to
  180 bar; a control panel on the car deck at the port side just aft of the ramp; levers for the visor bottom lock,
  side locks, parking plungers, visor, ramp hooks and bolts, and ramp; interlocks so the visor moves only with the
  ramp closed and the ramp opens only with the visor open.
- Car deck: twelve 4-inch scuppers each side, sprinklers, smoke detection, fans giving 20 air changes an hour.

Not found for any 1960s or 1970s ferry in this list, and therefore n/v: boilers, purifiers, compressors,
fresh-water generators, steering gear makes, shaft generators and tank capacities.

## 4. Life-saving appliances of the era

What the rules required:

- SOLAS 1960, as summarised by IMO: passenger ships on international voyages carry lifeboats on each side for 50
  per cent of all on board; some boats may be replaced by liferafts; additional liferafts for 25 per cent and
  buoyant apparatus for 3 per cent. The lifeboats were "the traditional open design, most of them without power"
  until the 1983 amendments required enclosed, engined boats [I].
- The detailed wording below is the pre-1983 Chapter III as reproduced at [A]. It is the 1974 convention text,
  which IMO says was at first similar to that of 1960. The 1960 text itself was not reached, so treat the detail as
  close but n/v for 1960.
- Lifeboat construction: open boats with rigid sides; at least 7.3 m long; no more than 150 persons or 20,300 kg
  laden; boats for more than 60 and up to 100 persons to be motor boats or have other approved mechanical
  propulsion; boats for more than 100 persons to be motor boats [A].
- Motor lifeboats: at least one on each side of a passenger ship; compression-ignition engine; 6 knots; fuel for
  24 hours [A].
- Emergency boats: two boats of at most 8.5 m, one each side, kept ready for immediate use at sea [A].
- Davits: each lifeboat on its own set; luffing or gravity type up to 2,300 kg in turning-out condition, gravity
  type above; launching against 15 degrees of list and 10 degrees of trim; wire falls and winches [A].
- Short international voyages, which is how these ferries were certified (Stena Danica of 1969 was [TQ]): sets of
  davits by registered length, with the smaller number allowed exceptionally in brackets [A]:

| Registered length, m | Sets of davits | Minimum lifeboat capacity, m3 |
|---|---|---|
| 70 to 75 | 5 (4) | 61 |
| 78 to 82 | 6 (5) | 76 |
| 91 to 96 | 8 (6) | 102 |
| 96 to 101 | 8 (6) | 110 |
| 101 to 107 | 9 (7) | 122 |
| 107 to 113 | 9 (7) | 135 |
| 119 to 125 | 10 (7) | 157 |
| 133 to 140 | 12 (9) | 185 |

- On short voyages liferafts make up the places the boats do not provide. The administration may reduce the boats
  to four, two each side, on ships of 58 m and over if boats and rafts together take everyone. In addition:
  liferafts for 10 per cent more, and buoyant apparatus for 5 per cent of all on board [A].
- Launching devices for liferafts on short voyages were at the administration's discretion [A].

What the ships carried:

- Photo counts [P]: two boats each side on Stena Danica (1965), Betula, Hamlet and Gotland; three each side on
  Skandia and Apollo (1970); three to four on Kapella and Fennia; about four on Stena Germanica and Tor Anglia;
  five on Prinsessan Christina. Registered length is shorter than length overall, so I compare loosely: six boats
  on a ship of about 96 m between perpendiculars (Apollo) equals the bracketed figure in the table.
- Skagerak (1966) was abandoned into lifeboats and "modern self-inflating rubber liferafts" [V].
- Viking Sally (1980) shows the end state of the same rules: ten open motor lifeboats of glass-reinforced plastic
  for 692 persons under davits on deck 8, boarded from deck 7; 63 inflatable rafts for 1,575 persons, twelve of
  them davit-launched from four davits; six rigid floats for 20 persons each, carried as SOLAS 1960 buoyant
  apparatus [E].
- Regula's boat deck, photographed in the 2000s: open boat under gravity davits, raft canisters on cradles [P].

Not verified for any named ship of the period: davit makers, boat lengths, boat material and exact raft numbers.

## 5. What makes the look right

All of this is my reading of photographs on faktaomfartyg [P].

- Hull form: strong sheer forward; raked, flared bow with a knuckle; the seam of the bow visor visible in the
  plating; anchors in pockets; a winged emblem or crest on the bow (Betula, Stena Danica 1965, Tor Anglia,
  Skandia). Open forecastle with windlass and capstans.
- Stern: a transom with a single top-hinged stern door (Betula, Prinsessan Christina, Tor Anglia) and open after
  decks stepping down in tiers above it.
- Openings: round portholes in the hull below the car deck; one or two rows of large rectangular windows along the
  saloon decks; a curved or faceted wheelhouse front with open bridge wings.
- Masts: a tapered pole foremast with a crosstree on the wheelhouse, and a second mast or exhaust post aft, on the
  early ships (Skandia, Betula, Hamlet, Peter Pan, Stena Danica 1965). From 1966 to 1970 a braced or lattice radar
  mast appears on the wheelhouse (Tor Anglia, Stena Germanica, Apollo 1970).
- Funnels, four period types: a low, wide streamlined casing just abaft the bridge (Stena Danica 1965, Gotland,
  Apollo 1970); twin funnels side by side aft (Fennia, Svea Regina, Tor Anglia); bare black exhaust posts
  (Prinsessan Christina); a raked funnel with fins (Stena Germanica). Skandia shows no conventional funnel: a round
  glazed deckhouse amidships and an exhaust mast aft.
- Boats: open white boats under gravity davits on the boat deck abreast or abaft the funnel, with orange-brown or
  green covers; white raft canisters on cradles between them.
- Liveries: Stena in 1965, white hull, red boot-topping, red funnel with white S and black top. Viking Line
  (Rederi AB Slite), red hull with white "VIKING LINE", white upperworks, red funnel. Sessanlinjen, white hull with
  the name in large letters. Linjebuss, blue hull with the white of the superstructure sweeping down toward the
  bow. Gotlandsbolaget, white hull and red funnel with white G. TT-Linie, white hull, yellow funnel with black top.
  Tor Line, dark hull with white "TOR LINE". Silja, white hull with a seal emblem.
- Details: ship's name in raised letters on the wheelhouse front (Betula); varnished wood caps on the rails and
  blue-painted steel decks with white benches on the sun deck (Regula in the 2000s); green-painted mooring deck
  (Apollo in 1997). Teak-laid decks are n/v.

## 6. Comparison for a walk-through model

Sourced items carry their key. Crew ranges and room counts for a new design are my estimates and are n/v.

| | about 70 to 80 m | about 100 to 110 m | about 125 to 135 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real models | Stena Danica 1965, Betula class | Apollo 1970, Skandia 1961, Stena Germanica 1967 | Prinsessan Christina, Fennia, Aallotar, Tor Anglia |
| Character | day ferry: saloons and shop, few or no cabins | complete overnight ferry of the late 1960s | toward the cruise ferry: pool, nightclub, conference rooms |
| Decks, tank top to wheelhouse | 6 [GA] | 9 [TA] | 8 [TQ, Wfi] |
| Vehicle decks | 1, with side galleries [D] | 1, with hanging decks; 4.20 m height [D] | 1, with platform or hanging decks [TQ, L] |
| Machinery spaces | main and auxiliary engine rooms, thruster room, steering gear [GA] | the same, with control room and emergency generator room [TA] | the same, with workshop; 11 watertight bulkheads [TQ] |
| Main engines | 2 to 4 [F] | 2 to 4 [F] | 2 to 8 [F] |
| Berths | 0 to 57 [F] | 108 to 385 [F] | 184 to 865 [F] |
| Public rooms | 4 saloons, shop, galley [GA] | 5 to 7 rooms, n/v | 10 or more rooms [B, L, Wfi] |
| Lifeboats | 4 [GA, P] | 6 to 8 [P] | 8 to 10 [P] |
| Crew | 47 on board Skagerak in 1966 [F] | n/v; my estimate 60 to 90 | room for 120 on Fennia [Wfi] |

Reasoning for the size decision:

- About 105 m is the best documented size: Apollo (1970) has a sourced nine-deck description, a sourced 4.20 m
  vehicle deck height, a sourced machinery arrangement and controllable-pitch propellers.
- It gives every period space an engineer would look for (two engine rooms, control room, thruster room, steering
  gear, emergency generator room) without hundreds of repeated cabins to model.
- The small class is quicker to build but has almost no night-ferry character. The large class has the richest
  machinery and public rooms but roughly doubles the modelling work.
- One drive-through main deck with hoistable side decks is what the period ships had at every size.
